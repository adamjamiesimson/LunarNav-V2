Methods and limitations

Useful analysis starts with transparent assumptions. Here is what powers the calculations — and what they cannot tell you.

Independent validation

The analytical Sun/Earth geometry was compared with NASA JPL Horizons results for three lunar sites across three seasonal windows. Maximum sampled errors were approximately 0.480° azimuth and 0.285° elevation. These are sample-specific results, not universal precision guarantees.

Open engineering

Model assumptions

  • Solar array: fixed and horizontal, no tracking; sunlight constant at 1361 W/m².
  • Electrical load is constant and the battery is idealised (no chemistry, no thermal behaviour, charge clamped to capacity).
  • No RF link budget: “geometric Earth visibility” is line-of-sight geometry only — not confirmed communication availability.
  • Visibility counts a body's center above the horizon; disc radius, refraction, eclipses and spacecraft shadows are not modelled.
  • Horizon precision is limited by terrain sampling (1° azimuth bins, DEM-resolution radial steps): very narrow occlusions can still be missed.
  • Ephemeris is a truncated analytical series (±~0.5° verified sample errors), not JPL/SPICE.

Terrain data (NASA LOLA)

Elevation source: NASA GSFC PGDA south-polar LOLA DEMs (Barker et al. 2023), 60–80 m/pixel, covering 60°S–90°S in MOON_ME south-polar stereographic coordinates. For each site or waypoint the app reads a ~40 km patch and converts elevation to a local horizon profile: along 360 azimuth directions (1° bins), out to a 20 km radius in steps matched to the DEM pixel size, the highest terrain elevation angle — including a spherical lunar curvature correction — becomes the horizon for that azimuth. The same patches feed the 3D globe's displaced terrain (a global LOLA/Kaguya SLDEM2015 grid covers 60°S–60°N for the wider overview; the north polar cap has no merged product and is labelled as uncovered). Relief exaggeration is a visual setting only and never affects calculations. Profiles are bundled per study site (exported by CI from the NASA COG); on-demand and local GeoTIFF loading cover custom coordinates. 60–80 m pixels and 1° bins cannot resolve small hazards or sub-degree gaps.

How these estimates work

LunarNav V2 computes Sun and Earth azimuth and elevation for user-selected lunar coordinates and UTC dates using an analytical model.

01

Celestial geometry

Truncated analytical lunar and solar orbital series (Meeus/NOAA style) are transformed into a Moon-fixed frame using the IAU lunar rotation model. Celestial direction is converted to local azimuth/elevation.

02

Visibility decision

A body is counted as visible when its center is above the 0° geometric horizon. Sun and Earth percentages are approximations sampled every two hours; simultaneous availability requires both conditions at once.

03

What is deliberately missing

Optional coarse NASA LOLA elevation pixels can produce a local 20 km horizon profile; by default calculations remain flat-horizon. The first-order energy and antenna screening model does not include exact surface properties, detailed charging physics, link margin, antenna pattern, terrain below DEM resolution, disk corrections or eclipses. The 3D globe is visual context and not the DEM.

04

Independent JPL benchmark

The analytical Sun/Earth geometry was compared with 90 independently returned JPL Horizons azimuth/elevation measurements across Mons Mouton, Malapert and an approximate Shackleton Rim coordinate in November 2026, March 2027 and July 2027. Maximum sampled errors were 0.480° azimuth and 0.285° elevation. These are not worst-case error guarantees, especially around the horizon where a small error can change a visible/hidden decision. Reference measurements are archived in GitHub.

05

Further validation

Increase the independent JPL/NAIF SPICE benchmark coverage, use shorter adaptive time steps at rise/set transitions, validate LOLA horizon projections, and add documented engineering uncertainty before representing these outputs as operational estimates.
